1. Arabica coffee has 44 chromosomes, while Robusta has 22 — Arabica is essentially a naturally occurring polyploid hybrid.
2. Coffee beans aren't actually beans: they're the seeds inside coffee cherries, and a single cherry usually contains two seeds.
3. Coffee can smell like berries, jasmine, wine, chocolate, or even tropical fruit without any of those flavors being added — they come from compounds created during genetics, fermentation, roasting, and brewing.
4. During roasting, coffee can lose roughly 15–20% of its mass, mostly because water and other volatile compounds escape.
5. The famous “coffee smell” is largely made of volatile compounds that can disappear rapidly after grinding, which is why grinding immediately before brewing matters so much.
6. Coffee cherries can be fermented with yeasts and bacteria, and controlled fermentation can dramatically change the final cup's flavor profile.
7. Carbon dioxide trapped inside freshly roasted coffee is one reason fresh coffee can behave wildly differently during brewing — especially during the bloom.
8. A coffee's roast level can change its density enough that the same scoop volume can contain noticeably different masses of coffee.
9. Espresso isn't inherently stronger because it's espresso — its defining feature is a concentrated extraction created by forcing hot water through finely ground coffee under pressure.
10. Water can make up around 98% of a brewed cup, meaning the chemistry of your brew water can radically change the flavor even when you use exactly the same beans and recipe.
11. Coffee's acidity isn't simply “bad sourness.” Specialty coffees can contain acids such as citric, malic, tartaric, and acetic acid, contributing fruit-like complexity.
12. The coffee plant's seeds develop inside a fruit, so coffee processing is essentially a food-science problem: the producer is controlling how a fruit is transformed before roasting.
13. “Natural” and “washed” coffees can taste radically different from the same variety because processing changes how the seed develops and which compounds survive into roasting.
14. Grinding coffee creates an enormous increase in exposed surface area. A whole bean can stay relatively stable, but once ground, oxygen can reach vastly more of the coffee at once.
15. Decaf coffee isn't completely caffeine-free. Modern decaffeination removes most of the caffeine, but a small amount normally remains.
